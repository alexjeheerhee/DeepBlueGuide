Why does "compared to normal" matter?

The ocean has absorbed more than 90% of the extra heat trapped by greenhouse gases, so sea surface temperatures are rising worldwide. Marine heatwaves (long stretches of unusually warm water) have become much more frequent since the 1980s.

Warmer-than-normal water can push fish toward cooler or deeper areas, change when they feed and spawn, and help some bacteria and parasites grow faster.

Sources: IPCC Special Report on the Ocean and Cryosphere (2019); NOAA OISST v2.1.

How is salinity changing, and why does it matter?

Melting ice adds fresh water. As glaciers and ice sheets melt, fresh water flows into the sea and dilutes it near the coast. In places like Greenland, layers of fresher water are building up at the surface, which scientists are watching because they could affect ocean currents.

Estuaries swing between salty and fresh. In bays where rivers meet the sea, like Chesapeake Bay, droughts make the water saltier and floods make it fresher. Climate change is making both droughts and heavy rain more extreme.

Fish and other life feel it. Many fish, crabs, and oysters can only live in certain salinity ranges. Changes in river flow can shift where they live, when they spawn, and how much food is available for their young.

Germs and parasites respond too. Some bacteria, such as Vibrio, grow best in warm, low-salinity brackish water. Sea lice on salmon, on the other hand, struggle in fresher water.

Sources: USGS Coastal Salinity Index; NOAA National Ocean Service; Chesapeake Bay Program.

Why do they react so differently?

Every species has its own comfort range. Each one has a range of temperature, salinity, and oxygen where it can grow, feed, and reproduce. Outside that range it gets stressed, and far outside it can die.

Moving away vs. staying put. Fish and crabs can swim away from bad water. Oysters are attached to the bottom and can only close their shells and wait.

The temperature-oxygen squeeze. In summer, the top of Chesapeake Bay can get too warm for striped bass while the deep water loses oxygen. Warm water holds less oxygen, so heatwaves and low oxygen often come together.

One species' good day is another's bad day. Warm, brackish water is great for Vibrio bacteria but stressful for striped bass. That's why scientists track many organisms, not just one.

Sources: Chesapeake Bay Program; Maryland DNR Eyes on the Bay; NOAA Chesapeake Bay Office; Coutant (1985), Transactions of the American Fisheries Society. Ranges are simplified for learning.

How to stay safe

  • 🩹 Cover cuts Stay out of salt or brackish water with a cut or new tattoo, or cover it with a waterproof bandage.
  • 🧤 Wear gloves Protect your hands when handling raw seafood or cleaning fish.
  • 🦪 Cook shellfish Don't eat raw or undercooked oysters and other shellfish.
  • 🧼 Wash wounds Clean any wound with soap and clean water after contact with seawater or raw seafood.
  • 🏥
    See a doctor fast If a wound gets red, swollen, or painful, get help right away and say you were in salt water.

⚠️ People with liver disease, diabetes, or a weakened immune system are at higher risk of serious illness.

What is Vibrio, and why is it spreading?

What it is. Vibrio are bacteria that live naturally in warm coastal water. Some kinds, like Vibrio vulnificus, can cause serious wound infections or illness from eating raw shellfish.

Why it is spreading. Vibrio grows faster in warm water and prefers low-salinity water where rivers meet the sea. As the ocean warms, the season with good conditions for Vibrio is getting longer, and the area is moving north.

What scientists have found. A study of the eastern U.S. found that V. vulnificus wound infections increased about eightfold from 1988 to 2018, and the area where cases happen moved north each year. Long-term plankton records from the North Atlantic also show Vibrio becoming more common as the water warmed.

Why it matters here. Chesapeake Bay is warm and brackish in summer, which is exactly the kind of water Vibrio prefers.

Sources: CDC, About Vibrio Infection; Archer et al., Scientific Reports (2023); Vezzulli et al., PNAS (2016).

How is climate change moving marine life?

Moving toward the poles. As the ocean warms, many fish follow the water temperature they prefer. Along the U.S. East Coast, species such as summer flounder and black sea bass have shifted north over the past few decades.

Changing the calendar. Warmer water and changing river flow can make salmon and trout migrate and spawn earlier. If young fish arrive before their food does, fewer of them survive.

Losing cold-water homes. Fish that need cold water, like trout, can lose habitat as streams warm. Moving fast does not always mean a species is safe: some shift quickly and still decline.

How are parasites changing, and is my fish safe to eat?

Warmer water speeds up many parasites. Water temperature controls how fast parasites like sea lice grow. Warming tends to tip the balance toward the parasite, especially where a fish species is at the warm edge of its range.

More hosts can mean more worms. Roundworms such as Anisakis and codworm finish their life cycle in whales, dolphins, and seals. Where these animals have recovered, and as ecosystems change, the worms are being found more often in Atlantic fish.

It depends on the place. Changes are not the same everywhere. In one region a parasite may increase while in another it disappears, so long-term local monitoring matters.

Eating fish safely. Cooking fish until the thickest part reaches 145°F (63°C) kills parasites. If you want to eat fish raw or lightly cured, it should be frozen first: at −4°F (−20°C) or colder for 7 days. A home freezer may not get that cold, so check yours or buy fish labeled for raw use.
